A Better-Than-GDD Plan for Olive Phenology in Catalonia
GDD's core weaknesses for olive (Olea europaea) are: (1) it ignores winter chilling, which olive requires to break floral bud dormancy; (2) it assumes a linear response with a single base temperature; (3) it ignores supra-optimal heat damage (>30 °C suppresses olive bud development); (4) it lumps cultivars; and (5) it ignores water stress, frost, and photoperiod modulation. Below is a layered plan that fixes each of these.

1. Target structure & label engineering
You have BBCH codes (phase_id 60 = beginning of flowering, 65 = full flowering, etc.). Don't model "DOY of stage X" as independent regressions — model the stage trajectory for each site-year.

Reformulate as time-to-event per stage transition: from a fixed biofix (e.g., Jan 1 or end-of-endodormancy) to the observed DOY of each BBCH code.
For the 1000 obs, build a long-format table: (site, year, cultivar, stage, observed_DOY, daily_weather[Jan1..DOY]).
Stages are ordered, so enforce monotonic constraints (DOY₆₀ ≤ DOY₆₅ ≤ DOY₆₉…) using either an ordinal regression or sequential survival framing.
2. Cultivar attribution from DUN + lat/lon
The Catalan DUN (Declaració Única Agrària) parcel data lets you replace "unknown cultivar" with a probabilistic assignment.

A better approach than simple GDD is a hybrid two-phase (chilling + forcing) process-based model, calibrated on your local data and enhanced with machine learning (ML) to incorporate cultivar, spatial, and additional weather variables. This directly addresses GDD’s core weaknesses: it ignores winter chilling requirements for dormancy release/flower induction, assumes linear temperature responses, overlooks cultivar differences, photoperiod, water stress, extremes, and spatial heterogeneity in Catalonia.

Recent Mediterranean olive research (especially in Italy, highly relevant to Catalonia’s climate) shows strong gains from chilling-forcing models (e.g., CAC_GDD or variants using Dynamic/Utah/Gaussian chilling) over pure GDD, with RMSE often ~4–8 days for flowering in validation. Your ~1000 PEP725 records (2013–2021, with phase_id likely BBCH codes such as 60 = beginning of flowering, 65 = full flowering) across multiple sites provide an excellent dataset for regional calibration—better than many published studies that rely on sparser data. Being able to identify cultivars (via DUN + lat/lon) is a major advantage, as requirements differ between early- and mid-late-budbreak types.

Recommended Hybrid Modeling Framework
Core physiological backbone: A CAC_GDD-style model (Chill and Anti-Chill days + Growing Degree Days).

Chilling phase (endo-dormancy release, typically Oct/Nov–Feb/Mar): Accumulate chill units (CU). Use multiple formulations and let data decide:
Traditional: Chilling Hours (<7.2°C), Utah model, Dynamic Model (Chill Portions—often most robust), DMA (De Melo-Abreu).
Improved: Gaussian-centered around ~11°C (optimal for olive flower induction per controlled experiments) with negation/anti-chill for warm interruptions (>15–17°C). Warm spells during winter can reduce effective chilling.
Forcing phase (eco-dormancy to observed stage): From the end of chilling (or a dynamic sprouting date), accumulate forcing units (GDD with optimized base ~5–6.5°C, or Growing Degree Hours). Upper threshold (~28–30°C) to avoid over-accumulation on very hot days.
Optimize parameters (chill threshold/requirement, GDD base, heat requirement, etc.) per cultivar group (or early vs. mid-late clusters) using genetic algorithms or similar optimizers on your observations. Start accumulation dates can be optimized or fixed (e.g., Nov 1 for chilling).
This is interpretable, physiologically grounded, and has shown superior performance (RMSE ~4 days for blooming in mid-late cultivars) vs. more complex models like PhenoFlex when data are scattered/limited, as in heterogeneous Mediterranean regions.

ML enhancement layer (the key upgrade for your dataset):

Use process-model outputs (accumulated CU at a date, predicted sprouting DOY, accumulated GDD from sprouting) as strong input features.
Add rich predictors from your daily weather + auxiliary data:
Cumulative precip, vapor pressure deficit (VPD), or soil moisture proxy over key windows (winter drought has limited direct effect in some studies, but spring water stress matters for flowering intensity).
Extremes: Frost days during sensitive periods, hot days (>30–35°C) that can damage flowers.
Photoperiod/daylength on the date.
Spatial: lat, lon, altitude (alt vs. alt_dem), distance to coast, slope/aspect (from DEM), climate/soil zone.
Cultivar (as categorical or one-hot; critical due to differing chill requirements).
Temporal: DOY, year (for trends), previous-year chilling or proxy for alternate bearing (fruit load memory strongly suppresses flowering even after cold winters—PEP725 may lack direct fruit data, so flag or proxy this).
Targets:
Primary: Regression for DOY of key stages (filter to phase_id = 60, 65, etc.).
Alternative: Predict current BBCH stage (classification/ordinal regression) given weather sequence up to a date, or time-to-event (survival analysis) for stage transitions.
Algorithms (start simple, scale up):
XGBoost, LightGBM, or Random Forest: Excellent baseline. Handles interactions, non-linearities, missing data; provides feature importance (you’ll likely see chilling accumulation, cultivar, and spring forcing as top predictors).
Mixed-effects models (if many repeated observations per site/cultivar) or GAMs for semi-parametric flexibility.
If restructuring as daily time series per site-year: LSTM/Transformer (with regularization; your ~1000 records likely correspond to fewer unique site-years, so use CV carefully).
Hybrid benefit: The process model captures the main thermal driver; ML captures residuals from additional drivers, microclimate variation across Catalonia (coastal, inland, pre-Pyrenees), and cultivar effects.

Yes — Pulling the Full PEP725 Olive Dataset Is a Major Upgrade
Going from ~1000 Catalan records to the full European olive corpus is probably the single highest-leverage change you can make. It transforms what's statistically identifiable, but it also introduces real hazards. Here's what changes and how to handle it.

1. What you actually gain
The full PEP725 olive holdings span roughly Portugal → Spain → southern France → Italy → Slovenia/Croatia → Greece → Cyprus, with records back to ~1950 in places. Realistic expectations:

Aspect	Catalonia only	Full PEP725 olive
Sample size	~1000	~10–50k observations
Climate gradient (winter chill)	~200–800 CU	~50–1500+ CU
Forcing gradient (spring GTU)	moderate	very wide
Cultivar diversity	~5–8 dominant	50+ named, many "unknown"
Years	2013–2021	1950s–present (sparse pre-1990)
Latitude range	40.5–42.5°N	~34–46°N
The big win: identifiability of the chilling response. With Catalonia alone, almost every site-year accumulates "enough" chill, so the chill requirement C
R
​
  is poorly constrained — the data only see the forcing side. Add Andalusia, Crete, Cyprus, and southern Italy, and you finally observe chill-limited site-years where mild winters delay or fail flowering. That's what lets you estimate the full chill→force response surface, not just its right half.

2. What changes in the model — concretely
(a) The functional coefficient surface becomes estimable
Recall β
k
​
 (t,u) in the Bayesian FGAM. With Catalonia only, the Nov–Jan portion (chilling window) is nearly flat in the data — your posterior would just return the prior. With pan-Mediterranean data, you'll get sharp, data-driven estimates of where chilling matters and where it saturates. This is the difference between a publishable methods paper and a regional case study.

(b) Hierarchy gets deeper
Move from a 2-level to a 4-level hierarchy:


observation
  └─ site-year
       └─ site (lon, lat, alt)
            └─ bioclimatic region   ← NEW (e.g., Köppen Csa/Csb/BSk)
                 └─ cultivar
                      └─ population (cultivar-region interaction)  ← NEW
Partial pooling across regions stabilises rare cultivars and prevents one country's recording bias from dominating.

(c) Cultivar attribution gets harder, not easier
DUN solves cultivar in Catalonia. Outside Catalonia you have no equivalent registry except patchy ones (Italy's SIAN, Spain's SIGPAC for some regions, Portugal's iDigital). For the rest:

Treat cultivar as fully latent with a region-conditional prior p(c∣country, NUTS-3) derived from FAO/IOC cultivar census tables.
Or marginalise cultivar entirely outside Catalonia and let cultivar enter only as a random intercept indexed by (country × NUTS-3) — coarser but honest.
Or use a "known/unknown" cultivar indicator and let the model learn separate variance components.
(d) Photoperiod becomes a real signal
Across 34–46°N, day length on April 1 ranges from ~12.4 h to ~13.0 h. Within Catalonia this variation is negligible; pan-Mediterranean it's identifiable. Add photoperiod as a functional covariate or as a multiplicative modifier on the forcing accumulator.

The most important comparison is:

Does the hierarchical global-plus-Catalonia model beat the Catalonia-only model on held-out Catalonia years and sites?

My recommended final design
I would build the model in this order:

Model 1: strong non-Bayesian benchmark
Use CatBoost or LightGBM with lag-window features:

day of year,
photoperiod,
chill features,
temperature windows,
precipitation windows,
VPD windows,
radiation windows,
cultivar,
site,
region,
year.
This gives you a strong predictive benchmark quickly.

Model 2: Bayesian functional ordinal model
Use spline-compressed lagged weather histories and ordinal likelihood.

This gives you interpretability and uncertainty.

Model 3: Bayesian hierarchical regional model
Add broader PEP725 olive data with:

cultivar random effects,
region random effects,
provider random effects,
site random effects,
year random effects.
Model 4: latent state-space model
Only if the data contain enough repeated observations per site-year.

Bottom line
Yes, adding more PEP725 olive data should help substantially.

But the best use is not naive pooling. Use a hierarchical Bayesian functional model:

broader PEP725 olive data learns the general olive phenology-weather relationship, while Catalonia-specific, cultivar-specific, site-specific, year-specific, and provider-specific effects adapt the model to your target region.

That gives you a much stronger alternative to GDD and should improve both prediction and biological interpretation.
